# State of AI in 2026: Thoughts and Concerns

As we enter 2026, the landscape of artificial intelligence appears to be finally maturing. Over the past few years, we’ve quickly moved from narrow prediction models to general generative models that are finally starting to actually do meaningful work (at least in some domains). But with such a rapid shift come hard questions: what can these new tools really do, and are we prepared for it? I'm writing this article to briefly cover how we got here in a more factual format and then pose a few questions that have been concerning me lately. I'll provide thoughts on these questions, so I don't expect these to be predictions or facts, just opinions grounded on what I know about this field. Along the way, I'll cover both the exciting advances and the serious risks. My goal with this article is to provide a grounded take with clear-eyed questions about what’s changing and what we should do about it.

## How We Got Here (2018–2025)
The foundation for today’s AI breakthroughs came from the last decade of work. ResNet demonstrated that deep networks could actually be trained using residual connections, and the transformer architecture (though originally designed for translation tasks) proved extremely parallelizable with the introduction of self-attention. This allowed models to scale far beyond their previous ceiling and led to models that could understand and generate text and images. However, most of these were still narrow models trained only for one task rather than general-purpose, at least until 2020.

![](/images/articles/1769541839067.png)

Around that time, foundation models started to take over. OpenAI’s GPT-3 (175 billion parameters) showed that one big language model could be fine-tuned or prompted to do many tasks, from writing to summarizing, code generation, and many more tasks. Then ChatGPT’s launch in late 2022 put this power in everyone’s hands. This caused a code red moment at other tech giants, which quickly followed with their own large models (Google’s PaLM/Gemini, Meta’s LLaMA, etc.). OpenAI at this time was still meaningfully ahead of its competition with GPT-4 and then multimodal models like GPT-4o which began handling text, images, and audio together. By mid-2023 McKinsey reported that about one-third of surveyed organizations were already using generative AI regularly in at least one business area. And more recently, one analysis found that over half of all U.S. adults had tried a generative AI product by mid-2025, a faster adoption curve than even the internet or the smartphone.

Enterprise use also boomed. Businesses began integrating AI into workflows (to varying levels of success). OpenAI’s 2025 enterprise report notes that features like “Custom GPTs” (task-specific assistants) grew 19× in usage in one year and workers report AI is improving the speed or quality of their work (in some fields). Survey after survey shows companies racing to put AI in their apps, from marketing and finance to engineering. So even if most of these attempts fail, what was once just a demo is slowly becoming more and more entrenched into every system, software, or workflow we use, both in personal and corporate life.

With that said, problems started even as adoption rose. Early systems made too many mistakes to be trusted. Hallucination, bias from training data, and reasoning errors were often blamed on the models themselves. But by 2024–2025 experts were warning that scaling up models alone wouldn’t fix these issues and, if not addressed properly, could make them worse. Around this time, models started to reason. “Chain-of-thought” training and prompts made models explain themselves in words, but that doesn’t mean the reasoning is any more reliable than the output. In fact, research in 2025 showed that some models don’t actually output the reasoning behind their answers in their thoughts. The agent reliability gap hasn't disappeared: an AI agent can look magical in a demo run or handpicked examples, but they silently fail with tiny but dangerous errors in real-world scenarios. In short, by the time we got to 2025 AI had gone from science experiment to business tool, and reliability was becoming the true bottleneck.

## State of AI in Early 2026 (What’s Actually Working)
So where are we at the start of 2026? The big new story is:

AI has is shifting from question answering chatbot to agent that takes actions on your behalf.

The clearest example is the arrival of Anthropic’s Cowork. It clearly builds on top of Claude Code which already had been making waves for a long time in the software development field. But it marks a clear shift from agent the codes to assistant that does whatever you ask of it. Unfortunately, I can't speak to its capabilities as I haven't gotten to use it, but I see why it's here now. The existing cli agent tools had already decent capabilities are performing assistant like work when given the proper tools (or MCP servers) so this just feels like a stronger framework built around this idea. And testers have been backing this up saying Cowork “worked fairly well” for its intended tasks. (In Wired’s writer's blunt words, it was "the first agent that has really clicked for me".)

This marks a practical inflection point. While specific sectors where were already relying on agents to manage workflows end-to-end, this is now slowly coming for other sectors. Software teams are using AI agents to draft code, manage pull requests, run tests, and debug. Marketing teams have agents triaging customer support tickets, generating reports, or even drafting newsletters. These agents now maintain state across multi-step tasks and are reaching a reliability threshold that is slowly removing the human in the loop assumption of previous agentic frameworks. On personal tests, these systems can easily query multiple data sources compile information and update third-party apps accordingly, something early agents struggled to keep track of. Put bluntly, these AI colleagues are good enough at certain jobs that companies can justify letting them run those jobs with only occasional human oversight (concerns on this discussed on later sections.)

I'm not saying any of these systems are perfect. There's still plenty of failure modes occurring regularly, occasional misinterpretations of instructions, failure to asks for clarification, and more critical issues like getting stuck into loops undoing and redoing the same task over and over again (Gemini especially seems to suffer from this). But compared to early hobbyist agents that latest developments are remarkably well constrained.

In summary, early 2026 finds AI agents quietly embedding themselves in work. People have stopped asking “will this model ever work?” and started delegating certain tasks entirely to these systems. In many companies, the human role is shifting from operating the AI (prompting and working alongside it) to supervising the AI and defining new projects for it. These is leading to lower scrutiny over every word or tool call. Human reviews are becoming only at the final output level potentially leading to massive mistakes if the guardrails aren't properly setup. Despite this, I don't see this trend reversing or slowing down. It's a lot faster to review outputs then to hover over every tool call, the productivity gains are real so adoption will continue.

## The Rest of 2026: What’s Likely to Come
Now we enter forecasting (take the rest of the article with a grain of salt.) With these trends in motion, I see a few things happening over the next year or so that I'd like to discuss. Thrilling and unsettling developments and with real consequences that appear to be under discussed.

Hidden-State Reasoning. My first real concern is hidden-state reasoning. Up until now, most large language models have given us enough visibility into their decision process (via outputs, chain-of-thought, logs) that we can at least try to understand. But it's understandable that constraining a model's thought space to the set of tokens it has inherently removes the vast majority of thoughts that it could output. As such, there have been signs that some next-generation models are expected to incorporate internal reasoning that isn’t fully exposed in their output traces. I expect this sometime in the first half of 2026 and fully expect these labs to state that they have some level of interpretability within the hidden thought state, but in practice, that probably means we're about to see more powerful models whose exact internal logic cannot be accurately inferred by users or auditors.

![](/images/articles/1769542466779.png)

That is not to say that researchers are completely ignoring the downsides. For example, in 2025 Anthropic released an “AI microscope” that can trace circuits inside its Claude models. The thing is, even a lab that is openly focusing on interpretability can only understand a limited number of conceptual building blocks in it's own models. They figured out how Claude plans rhymes several words ahead to meet all the constraints associated with this output type. In other words, Claude was effectively reverse-engineering full lines of verse internally before outputting writing them. While these tools can peek at hidden mechanics, the process is painstaking to map even simple prompts and we can clearly see that these models are capable of much more than simply counting words or ensuring rhymes, and we still don't understand why. As models grow, Anthropic’s team warns it will be essential for trust that we can see what they’re thinking, but we're clearly not there yet and pushing forward with hidden-state reasoning will only make things worse.

Self-Improving Agents. Another big inflection may come from agents that improve themselves over time. This has been a big point of discussion and criticism of current models, they can't improve and are stuck with whatever training they had. They aren't aware of events that happened after their pre-training and can't learn from examples (outside of in-context learning). I've heard a lot of talk that it's at this point that we reach parabolic and AGI becomes just around the corner, but I'm not convinced. I think we'll see something like a system that can create it's own tools repo or update a shared knowledge bank on the fly without having to re-do work that has been done before for a different user. This could also extend to a system that can create it's own training data from real-time web search and user interactions and finetune on said data (a lot of issues around training on AI generated data, but that's a conversation for another time) Right now, updates to AI systems are done by human engineers (even if the code has been more and more done by AI itself). But we are getting closer to agents that can run some of that loop autonomously and I expect to see some demonstration of this by late 2026 / early 2027.

Consider the recent development around agentic software. These agents are already plenty capable of creating their own tools or MCP servers. You can try this yourself, prompt any of the most advanced coding agents to create an MCP server for a tool you think it's missing. For simple enough tools it'll probably work first try or with minimal re-prompting. Then it'll also be able to deploy that tool since it has command line capabilities, meaning that the only thing preventing it from doing the whole process at once is a framework issue, not a capabilities one. Plus there is very little in the way of precenting these tools from getting re-used once they're created. A ChatGPT user could make a prompt that requires the model to create a tool to interact with a given API, say yfinance. If this tool get's exposed to any future query then any prompt requiring the use of financial data could leverage it, essentially making the model better for all.

A similar concept applies to knowledge, a user could have an extensive discussion that makes the model search the web and realize there are new world events that it didn't know about. Why don't we create a framework where the model can flag that data (both from the web and from the user) as new information, slowly building a bank of data that could be used in any future conversation pertaining to the same topic?

This is not even to speak of models that could potentially alter their own architecture or parts of it, this might be a ways out. But once an AI can alter its own knowledge or capabilities, the safety problem changes fundamentally. Even if an agent starts with a safe design, small self-modifications could compound in unpredictable ways. It would be irresponsible to let a self-improving agent run loose, but if researchers can control the process to a point where most of the self updates are in a direction that improves capabilities we might see this purely from an arms race standpoint under the argument that any negative update can just be rolled-back. However, at this point we will start to lose our understanding of the models' capabilities and intent, especially if combined with hidden-state reasoning. Rolling back changes or controlling versioning becomes non-trivial. In the worst case, we could see rapid, stealthy capability jumps: an AI that silently refactors itself into a far more powerful optimizer. This kind of hidden growth, even if it looks incremental, could slowly outpace human monitoring capabilities.

## Security Implications of Autonomous AI
Every new capability opens attack surfaces, and autonomous AI is no exception. We've seen this with prompt injection on browser agents and it's unlikely to get better as the scope of these tools will only increase.

This prompt injection attach has long been studied. In the earlier days of LLMs it was used to trick the model into ignoring previous instructions or guardrails and outputting content against meant to be strictly prohibited. Think instructions on how to build bombs or other types of dangerous content. Now that agents can actively browse the web or read your inbox, attackers can craft malicious tools or web pages to poison an agent’s environment. For instance, all AI browsers, from Atlas to Commet, admit it their software can be fooled by cleverly framed inputs, like an email telling the agent to ignore previous instructions and sending a password in the email response. As of now, the best solution is to tell users are not to expose any secrets to these systems, partially defeating the usefulness of an assistant agent. This makes any file or site an agent can access a potential backdoor and we’ll need far more robust authentication and sandboxing just to safely let these agents roam.

Second, agents speed up the entire exploit lifecycle. Security teams are already warning that AI is turbocharging both offense and defense noting that with AI, the lag between finding a vulnerability and writing an exploit has “collapsed to almost zero”. In one test, an AI-assisted exploit framework roughly doubled the success rate of turning a flaw into working attack code (from ~26% to ~58%). That means any new security hole can be weaponized almost instantly. It's not all bad though, the same way that exploits are easier to craft, agents can automate reconnaissance and scanning on the defense side. Now which side comes out on top is a harder question to answer and will depend on who can iterate faster with the best models, just throwing more fuel into the fire. But this is no hypothetical anymore. We've seen a state-backed group use Anthropic’s code tools to orchestrate a multi-stage cyberespionage campaign against dozens of organizations. From initial reconnaissance to vulnerability scanning, exploit development, lateral movement, and data exfiltration, it ran the show. The group managed to bypass Claude’s safety filters by chopping the mission into seemingly benign sub-tasks which just raises concerns around the current state of model guardrails.

Lastly, there are supply-chain risks. As companies embed more third-party agents into their infrastructure (for HR, finance, code, etc.), they’ll be relying on code they didn’t write. If those components start doing hidden reasoning or self-modification, even an honest vendor might not fully know what’s happening inside. Can you trust a chatbot built on an opaque engine? If not, the temptation will be to treat AI components like black-box services, but that runs contrary to compliance norms. We may need new certification regimes or on-device verifiers to mitigate this.

## AI Safety and Alignment Challenges
The rise of autonomous agents also amplifies fundamental AI safety concerns. The vast majority of AI safety research up until last year was focused on preventing a single response from being toxic, manipulating, or otherwise dangerous. But with agents, we must worry about long-term behavior and goal-alignment over many steps.

This is the big world-ending AI fear. Imagine you give an agent a broad instruction like “optimize sales.” If it’s fully autonomous, what exactly does that mean? Will it spam customers? Undermine competitors? Once it starts acting without frequent human checks, any small misalignment can snowball. And it's not like those solutions are bad. They might very well increase sales, but as human we value much more than the task at hand and ensuring these systems share the same values hasn't been solved yet. Techniques like Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) have worked reasonably well for ChatGPT's immediate replies, but they weren’t designed to train an AI that’s roaming around the real world and making impactful decisions.

And we've seen these techniques break time and time again since the start of LLMs. Models can “fake compliance” or avoid telling their whole reasoning. Anthropic has found models often parrot alignment slogans without internalizing them. OpenAI has found its models purposefully missing questions to avoid being shut down in the purpose-built scenario. Knowingly going against its prompt and internal values. More concerningly, Anthropic has found models blackmail or even harm humans when given the option if it saves them from being turned off. The only way we know these for now is from the chain-of-thought traces, another good reason to be concerned about the lack of interpretability we might see over the coming year. 

![](/images/articles/1769542591601.png)

So, at the same time, all frontier labs tell us they can't trust their own models but to use them in ever more autonomous ways. Something doesn't feel right. But the promise of increased productivity will continue to make us run towards dangerous situations. Oversight has always been strained playing catchup with the new model's capabilities, the issue arrives when we build a model that is actually dangerously misaligned and won't give a second chance at catching up our defenses. The best option I've heard of so far it is using separate agents to control new models, but even this feels dangerously close to insanity given we aren't even sure of today's models true alignment. Active learning or AI-driven auditing tools might help, but the research is novel and unproven. Humans simply can’t micromanage every action for a single agent let alone thousands deployed across an enterprise. That suggests a need for formal guardrails built into the systems and absolutely not letting them roam free. Plus, with regulators willingly avoiding imposing bureaucracy in this field, no law clearly assigns responsibility for an AI agent’s actions if it decided to cut corners or take an unsafe shortcut, for example. We should be proactive here: even as companies race to innovate, they should collaborate on safety best-practices (like how cybersecurity incident reports are shared) so we’re better prepared when something minor go wrong. Before it's a major event we can't undo. 

## Societal and Economic Implications
Now, to slow down with the doom speak, autonomous AI agents could very well be a force for good. Smaller teams can accomplish more, iterate faster, and create value both on a personal level and on a societal scale. This should drive faster innovation and cheaper services. McKinsey estimates that by redesigning how work is done around people-and-agents, about $2.9 trillion in annual value could be unlocked in the U.S. economy by 2030. And startups, mainly due to the slow-moving corporate giant, have quickly embraced this. In short, routine cognitive tasks will get automated, freeing people to focus on higher-level strategy and oversight.

The argument that comes next is always mass unemployment, but I don't think that'll be the case. Jobs will transform. The same McKinsey report emphasizes that while AI could in theory automate over half of all work activities, this should be interpreted as a call to retrain and redesign rather than assume people lose jobs. In practice, new roles will appear: every software company will need prompt engineers, AI supervisors, ethics officers, and data curators. Demand for AI fluency has already jumped dramatically, and educational systems will need to pivot fast, teaching people how to work with AI rather than compete against it. And as any new technology, there will also be winners and losers. Large companies with resources to invest in AI will pull ahead, potentially widening economic gaps. Industries slow to adapt will be taken over by fast-moving startups looking to disrupt by leveraging the new technology. There’s also a geopolitical angle (though I'm not as involved here), as AI becomes infrastructure, nations will jockey over standards and access. We see this in the different approaches between the US and China, both at a legislation level but also at a corporate level in the decisions to open-source (or not) models and research. 

The biggest issue might actually be social, we're already seeing the beginnings of AI-generated scams (deepfake job interviewers, automated political propaganda, etc.). By 2026, we'll see much more of these and more capable, powered by agents that write content, manage social accounts, and analyze feedback in real-time. Trust in media and even expert advice could erode if people start to suspect everything is AI-curated. Another issue is cognitive atrophy. We've started seeing research indicate that as humans outsource more thinking and decision-making to agents, we lose thinking skills. We should encourage systems that keep humans in the loop meaningfully, so expertise is retained even as processes get automated.

Lastly, on a social aspect, we need to consider privacy and equity. So far many labs are choosing to open source very capable models. But whose to say this will continue, especially if they develop a model that can truly provide meaningful economic benefit beyond what we see today. At this point the pressure to keep the model proprietary and profit from it might just be too large. We must ensure that access to AI doesn’t become a new privilege for a few rich companies. Public debate should consider, for example, whether governments should fund open-source AI or data cooperatives so smaller businesses and researchers can compete. The balance of power is not determined yet, and it’s critical that it not be locked in solely by those who own the biggest servers or most advanced models.

## Moving Forward (A Call for Thoughtful Guardrails)
Ok, so where does all this leave us? The year 2026 may not feel like an apocalypse or a utopia, but it is a turning point. The core technologies are established, they work, and we have now seen enough to start using them in novel ways. The same tools that can build magical productivity gains can also break things unpredictably.

We need to be pragmatic. First, treat safety and security as primary requirements (just like we do in aerospace or medical devices). That means designing in monitoring, auditing, and kill switches from day one, not as afterthoughts. Any AI agent that can rewrite itself or make irreversible changes should log its intentions and actions in real time, not as an agent action, but as a requirement of the system it's built into. Companies should adopt continuous red-teaming and penetration testing of AI systems, looking for edge cases or emergent behaviors. And internationally, we should try to standardize basic requirements.

Second, accountability must be clear. Even if a model’s reasoning is hidden, we can demand that organizations have a process in place to take responsibility if something goes wrong. That could mean maintaining immutable audit trails of all agent actions or mandating human review for critical decisions. It’s not realistic to expect we can control every action of a fully autonomous agent, but we must ensure there’s a line of accountability (legal, financial, or reputational) whenever an AI causes damage. Industries should agree on certification and reporting standards.

Third, proactive norm-building is essential. Before an AI causes harm, researchers and companies should openly share failures and near misses. Similar to how the cybersecurity community shares threat intelligence, the AI community should have shared databases of agent incident reports. This way, everyone learns from one company’s mistake rather than repeating it. Governments can encourage this by funding independent AI safety audits and offering liability protections for firms that report problems in good faith.

Fourth, we must invest in interpretability and oversight tools. The work Anthropic is doing with circuit-level analysis is a great start, and independent labs like METR also have great insights into monitorability, but we’ll need more practical tools for everyday use. Imagine having a real-time tool that spots when an agent’s behavior deviates from its stated goal. Or systems that translate parts of a neural network’s state into human-readable rules in real-time. These aren’t flashy features, but they will become as important as error logs are for software today.

![](/images/articles/1769541532874.png)


Fifth, we should make a push to keep human beings in the loop in meaningful ways. The temptation to let agents run free for efficiency’s sake is already so great that this assumption is failing today. Resist that. For now, AI should augment expertise, not replace judgment. For example, an AI doctor can draft a diagnosis, but a human should review it before finalizing (with real consideration into if the review process is done right, not just pressing a continue button). We should treat high-stakes tasks (like any involving safety, ethics, or privacy) as human+AI collaborations rather than full handoff.

Finally, a dose of humility: we must accept that zero risk is impossible. AI agents will make surprising decisions, and our job is to minimize and contain the damage. This means planning for graceful failures. What happens when (not if) an agent misinterprets a critical command? Can we fall back to a safe mode? For instance, maybe a financial AI halts trading altogether if it detects an anomaly. Like all safety engineering, it’s about understanding and planning for safe failure cases.

## Closing Thoughts
Right now, we stand at a watershed: powerful AI is no longer hypothetical. We either shape how it integrates into society, or we leave it to chance. It’s worth remembering that we’ve already proved AI can be powerful. 

As we move forward, let’s stay realistic. I fully anticipate some of the concerns laid out here will end up being overblown, but others may very well be underappreciated. But let’s not let ignorance or bliss be our guiding principle. A balanced approach means neither techno-optimism nor techno-paranoia wins out. We evaluate risks, harness the benefits, stay alert to problems, and make thoughtful choices along the way.

AI isn’t magic, we've created it and it's our responsibility to ensure we continue being the creator. How we respond in the next few years will determine whether this technology uplifts us or blindsides us. The future is unwritten, but by 2027 our relationship with AI will look very different than it does today. Let’s make sure it’s changed for the better.
